What happens to your log

The short version: what you write down is yours, it is shown to the people you chose and to nobody else, nothing about you is measured or sold, and you can take all of it out or delete all of it from inside the app.

Last updated 20 September 2026

Rooms is a place to write down the films, television, books and games you have finished, and to see what a few friends made of the same things. This page says exactly what it keeps, where that sits, who else ever sees it, and how to end it. It is written to be read rather than to be defensible.

What Rooms keeps

All of it is something you typed or tapped. There is no category here you did not put there yourself.

To have an account at all

  • Your email address. Held by the sign-in service, not in the app's own tables. It is used to send you a six-digit code or a confirmation link, and to let you back in. It is never shown to other people, and it is not used to send anything else.
  • A password, if you set one, so you do not have to wait for an email. It is stored hashed by the sign-in service; nobody, including us, can read it back.
  • Your handle — the short name other people see — and a line under your name if you write one. Every handle you have ever had stays yours, including ones you have changed away from. That is deliberate: if an old handle went back into the pool, somebody could take the name your friends know you by and be mistaken for you.
  • An avatar, which is three choices: a shape, a pair of eyes and a colour. It is stored as those three values and drawn by your phone. Rooms has no way to accept a photograph of you and never asks for one.

What you log

  • Entries: the title, your score, the date, anything you wrote about it, the parts you singled out, and the tickboxes for that kind of thing — first watch or rewatch, at the cinema or at home, paper or audio, finished or dropped.
  • Your shelf and any other lists, including the date each thing went on, and the order if you arranged them.
  • Your ten — the titles in it and their positions.
  • What you are in the middle of, and where you are up to if you said.
  • Anyone you have blocked. Kept so the block keeps working. You can see and undo your own list in settings; nobody can see a list of who has blocked them, because that is a list of people to go and find.
  • Which episodes you have watched. A tick, not an opinion — the thing that answers "where was I". It is yours alone: nobody else can see how far through something you are, and it is not shown in any feed.
  • Handovers: what you passed to somebody, what they passed to you, any line that came with it, and whether it was taken up.
  • Who you follow, and the likes you gave to other people's entries.
  • Rooms and clubs you are in, and how you voted in a club.
  • When each of those happened. Times are how the feeds are ordered and how "new since you last looked" is worked out.

Kept on your device only

These never leave the phone or the browser you are using. They are settings, not records.

  • Your theme, which tab you were on, the filters and chips you last picked on the spinner.
  • A two-letter country code, so "where can I watch this" can answer for the right country. It is guessed from your phone's language setting unless you change it, and it is sent with a streaming-availability lookup and nowhere else.
  • A key or a proxy address, if you chose to put in your own.
  • Anything queued while you were offline, until it goes through.
  • If you use Rooms without an account, the entire log — all of the above sits in your browser and no server ever sees it.

What Rooms does not keep

  • No analytics. There is no analytics service in the app, no tag manager, no session recorder, no crash reporter, no advertising kit, no pixel and no beacon. Nobody is counting your taps, because nothing is there to count them.
  • No tracking across other apps or sites, and nothing is shared with a data broker. There is no advertising in Rooms, so there is no profile to build for it.
  • Nothing is sold. Not now, and if that ever changes it is a different app with a different notice and your consent first, not a quiet edit to this page.
  • No location. The app never asks for it and could not use it.
  • No contacts, camera, microphone or photo library. The app asks for none of these permissions.
  • No adverts, so no advertising identifier.

Reporting, and blocking

If somebody is being awful, there is a Report link on their entry and on their profile. It asks what kind of thing it is and lets you add a line. A person reads every report, within a day, and acts on it — the offending thing comes down and, where it warrants it, the account goes. Reports are not readable through the app by anybody, including whoever sent one.

What we keep when you report: who you are, who or what it is about, which reason you picked, and anything you typed. If you later close your account the report stays, because it is about somebody else's behaviour rather than about you.

Blocking is immediate and needs nobody's agreement. It works both ways: you stop seeing anything they write, and they stop seeing anything you write. Any following between you ends, and anything either of you handed the other goes. It is undone in settings.

Blocking is enforced by the database rather than by the app being polite — a blocked person's rows are not sent to your device and yours are not sent to theirs. It is not a curtain over something still being delivered.

Some words will not go up. A short list of slurs is refused at the point of writing, in entries, handover notes, handles and bios. It is a crude instrument: it cannot read a sentence, it will miss anything said carefully, and it is not moderation. Reporting and blocking are what actually work. It is there so nothing awful goes up by accident. The list can be added to without updating the app, so a word it does not yet know can be answered the same day.

An invite link

Somebody sent a link to a room can see, before making an account, what they were sent to: the room's name, who is in it, and how many things those people have logged between them. Not a word anybody wrote. The code is fair proof that a room is real; it is not a licence to publish what is inside it to anyone who is handed six characters.

Who else can see what you wrote

This is the part that matters most, because Rooms is a thing you do with other people.

  • Anyone in a room with you sees the entries you put in that room: the score, what you wrote, and the parts you singled out. That is what a room is for.
  • Anyone at all can see an entry you did not mark room-only, and can like it. When you post, the app says which of the two you are doing, and you choose.
  • People who follow you see your entries in their feed on the same rule.
  • Anyone in a room with you can see what you are in the middle of, and that you are in the room right now, while you have it open. That last one is live and stops the moment you close the app — it is not stored.
  • A season or an episode you rated shows on that show's page under the same rule as anything else you wrote — but it never enters a feed. Only the show itself does. One person's week of television is not everybody else's week.
  • Whoever you hand something to sees that you handed it and any line you sent with it.
  • Your handle, your line and your avatar are visible to anyone who can find you.

A like is visible to the person whose entry it was and to anyone else who can see that entry. Names are shown — "mo and two others liked this" — so nothing about a like is anonymous.

Where it is, and who holds it

Four companies are involved, and it is worth being exact about what each one gets.

Supabase holds the log

The database, the sign-in and the live connections. They hold everything in the two lists above that is not device-only, including your email address. The project is hosted in Sydney, Australia. They process it on our instructions and do not use it for anything of their own.

Cloudflare looks things up

A small proxy that asks the film, book and game sources about titles, so that the sources never see you. It caches answers about titles — never anything about a person — and receives ordinary web request logs in the course of serving the request.

GitHub serves the page

Rooms is a single file served by GitHub Pages. GitHub sees the request for that file, including your IP address, as any web host would.

TMDB, Open Library and IGDB the covers

Searches go through the proxy, so these services do not see you. Cover images are the exception: posters and jackets load straight from those services to your device, so they can see your IP address and which covers your device asked for. We would rather that were not true and it may change; today it is true and you should know it.

Nothing else is loaded from anywhere else. The type and the sign-in library are served from the app's own address rather than from a content delivery network, on purpose, so that opening Rooms does not announce you to a company you have never heard of. There is a test in the repository that fails if anybody adds one back.

If you are in the UK or the EU

Your log is held in Australia, which the European Commission has not declared to have equivalent data protection law, and which the UK has not either. That makes storing it there an international transfer, and it needs a safeguard rather than an assurance.

The safeguard is the data processing agreement with Supabase, which includes the European Commission's Standard Contractual Clauses and the UK's addendum to them. It is the standard mechanism and it binds them contractually to protect your data to the same standard it would have in Europe. You can ask for a copy of what we have signed.

In practice what crosses is the same small set of things listed above — your email address, your handle and what you wrote down. No special-category data is collected by Rooms at all.

How long it is kept

Until you delete it. An entry stays until you remove it, and your account stays until you close it. Nothing expires on its own and nothing is archived somewhere you cannot reach.

Two practical exceptions. Backups of the database exist for a short window, so something deleted can persist in a backup for a few days before ageing out. And caches — the proxy's, your browser's, other people's copies of a feed — clear on their own timers.

Bringing a log in

Rooms can be used on a device with no account at all, in which case the whole log sits in that browser and no server ever sees it. Settings → Bring another log into this one takes the file that log writes and adds it to this one. What comes across is what is yours alone: your entries, your shelf, your ten, what you were in the middle of, and which episodes you had ticked. Nothing here is replaced, and anything you had already logged on the same day is left as it is.

What cannot come is everything about other people — rooms, follows, clubs, likes, handovers. Those are not yours to carry, and in a log with no account they refer to nobody.

Taking it out

In Settings → Download everything. It is one file, in JSON, containing every table your account touches: your profile, your entries, your lists and when things went on them, your ten, your rooms and who is in them, your clubs and votes, your likes, what you are in the middle of, which episodes you have watched, your handovers, who you have blocked, every handle you have held, and who you follow. It restores into a device-only log, so it is a file that opens again rather than a file that proves a point.

Closing it for good

In Settings → Close your account. You type your own handle to confirm, because this cannot be undone and there is no copy kept anywhere.

What actually happens, exactly:

  • Your account is deleted, and every row that hangs off it goes with it — entries, lists, your ten, likes, follows, what you are in the middle of, which episodes you had watched, handovers, who you had blocked, club memberships and votes.
  • The handles you held stop being yours and stay out of use. They are kept with no owner rather than freed, because a name your friends knew you by is not a name somebody else should be able to put on the day after you leave.
  • You are taken out of every room you were in.
  • A room you started stays open if anybody else is still in it. The room and the other people's entries are theirs, not yours, and taking them would be taking something from them.
  • A room with nobody left in it closes behind you.
  • Anything stored on the device you did it from is wiped, and the app puts you out.

Take your log out first if you want it. Afterwards there is nothing to ask us for.

What you can ask for

Depending on where you live you may have the right to see what is held about you, correct it, have it deleted, get a copy in a portable form, object to how it is used, or complain to a regulator. The app does the first four itself, immediately, without asking anyone — that is what Download everything and Close your account are. For anything else, or if either of those does not work for you, write to the address at the top and we will answer within a month.

The lawful bases, where that framing applies: performing the agreement to give you the app for everything needed to run your account and show your entries to the people you chose; legitimate interests for keeping the service working and secure; and consent, asked for separately, for anything that ever falls outside those. There is no legitimate-interests claim hiding any marketing or profiling here, because there is none.

Children

Rooms is not for children under 16, and accounts are not knowingly created for them. If you believe a child has made one, write to the address at the top and it will be removed.

If something goes wrong

Access is enforced by the database itself rather than by the app being polite — a row you are not allowed to read is not sent to your device in the first place. If a breach happens anyway that puts you at risk, you will be told, and so will the relevant regulator, without waiting to be asked.

Changes to this page

The date at the top changes when this does. If a change means Rooms starts doing something materially different with what you wrote, you will be told in the app before it takes effect, not after. The history of this page is public in the repository, so you can read what changed and when.
